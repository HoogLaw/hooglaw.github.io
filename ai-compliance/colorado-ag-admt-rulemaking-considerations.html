What the Attorney General Is Actually Asking About Colorado’s AI Rules

By Michael Hoog  |  August 1, 2026
Photograph by Michael Hoog
← Colorado AI Compliance

In June 2026 the Colorado Department of Law published a document called Pre-Rulemaking Considerations for the Automated Decision-Making Technology (Senate Bill 26-189) and Chatbot Safety (House Bill 26-1263) Acts. It collected informal public comment through July 13, 2026.

Almost nobody read it. It is worth reading, because the questions a regulator asks before writing rules tell you more about the direction than the rules themselves will once they arrive.

Where the process actually stands

No rules exist yet. The statute requires the Attorney General to adopt rules clarifying the post-adverse-outcome disclosure requirements on or before January 1, 2027, which is also the day the Act takes effect.

The Department described a two-phase approach. The first phase, informal input, closed July 13. The second phase, formal notice-and-comment rulemaking under the Colorado Administrative Procedure Act, was slated to begin at the end of summer 2026 with a notice of rulemaking and draft regulations attached. That phase includes at least one formal hearing and continued written comment, and everything submitted during it lands in the rulemaking record automatically.

Which means draft regulations are the next visible event, and they are close.

The mandate is narrow. The questions are not.

This is the part worth noticing. The statute only obligates the Attorney General to write rules about post-adverse-outcome disclosures. The considerations paper ranges much further, and the first substantive section asks how regulations could clarify the definitions of “ADMT,” “materially influence,” and “consequential decision.”

Those three terms are the entire coverage test. A business that is confident today about whether it is covered is confident about definitions the regulator has openly put on the table.

The Department also asked whether guidance would help distinguish ADMTs from technologies that “simply summarize, organize, or present information,” whether the developer and deployer definitions are sufficient, and whether there are other roles that should be accounted for. That last one is quietly significant for anyone sitting between a vendor and an end user, like an implementation consultant or a staffing agency.

Five stated principles

The Department set out five principles it says will guide the rules: promote consumer rights, clarify ambiguities, facilitate efficient and expeditious compliance, harmonize with other state, national, and international frameworks, and allow for innovation without unduly burdening entities.

Two of those cut toward businesses. The harmonization principle in particular suggests the Department is not looking to build something idiosyncratic, and a company that has already aligned to an existing framework is unlikely to be starting over.

The question small businesses should care about most

The Act lets a consumer request meaningful human review of an adverse decision “to the extent commercially reasonable.” The considerations paper asks what should determine that:

“What factors should be considered in determining what human review requirements are ‘commercially reasonable’? To what extent should factors such as the type of decision, volume of decisions, organizational capacity or size, cost, technological feasibility, or others play a role, if at all?”

Read that again with organizational capacity or size in mind. There is no small business exemption anywhere in SB 26-189. But the Department is openly asking whether size and capacity should shape what human review a business owes. That is the closest thing to accommodation for smaller employers anywhere in this statute, and it is being decided right now rather than settled.

It is not relief you can count on. The paper is explicit that its questions do not indicate the Attorney General is predisposed to any position. But if you run a small Colorado business and you were going to say something during formal rulemaking, this is the sentence to say it about.

Human review has more structure than people realize

The paper restates the statutory definition, and it is more demanding than the phrase suggests. Meaningful human review means review by an individual designated by the deployer with authority to approve, modify, or override the decision, who considers available relevant primary evidence, is trained, does not default to the system output, and has access to sufficient information to understand the relevant aspects of the ADMT.

The open questions are about how that gets measured. The Department asked what standards should determine whether review was meaningful, what training and authority reviewers need, whether specific timelines should be required, and whether requirements should differ where qualified reviewers are scarce.

It also asked whether deployers should face a deadline for responding to requests for personal data and correction instructions. The statute sets 30 days for the adverse outcome notice but does not clearly clock the response to a consumer request. Expect the rules to close that gap. The notice requirements article covers what the statute requires today.

Sector-specific rules are expected, not speculative

The Act itself says the content and format of post-adverse-outcome disclosures “may vary across consequential decision domains,” and that the General Assembly intends them to be further clarified through rulemaking accounting for sector-specific practices.

The Department asked what tailored guidance would help for education enrollment or an education opportunity; employment or an employment opportunity that creates or may create an employer-employee relationship; the lease or purchase of residential real estate in Colorado; a financial or lending service; insurance, including underwriting, pricing, coverage, and claims adjudication; health-care services; and essential government services and public benefits, including eligibility and renewal determinations. So a hiring disclosure and a lending disclosure will probably not look alike, and building one generic template for everything is likely wasted effort. Build for the domains you actually operate in.

The chatbot law rides along

The same document covers House Bill 26-1263, the Chatbot Safety Act, passed May 11, 2026. It reaches operators of publicly available conversational AI, with obligations around estimating user age, disclosing that the user is talking to a machine, protecting minors from sexually explicit content and from responses that simulate emotional dependence, providing account and privacy controls, maintaining suicide and self-harm response protocols, and not presenting outputs as equivalent to licensed professional services. Operators also file an annual report with the Attorney General.

The Chatbot Safety Act does not require rulemaking. The Attorney General intends to write rules anyway, under general discretionary authority, on the view that compliance obligations should be clear.

That matters for a Colorado business only if you run a public-facing conversational assistant. If you do, you are looking at a second compliance regime on a parallel track, and it is not covered by anything else on this page.

What to do with this

Three things.

  • Do not treat the definitions as settled. If your coverage analysis turns on a close reading of “materially influence,” write down why you reached your conclusion. That reasoning is what you will revisit when the rules land, and it is what demonstrates good faith if anyone asks.
  • Watch for the draft regulations. They were expected at the end of summer 2026 and they come with a formal comment period and at least one hearing. That is a real opportunity to be heard, and small businesses almost never take it.
  • Build the parts that will not change. Your inventory of tools, your designated reviewer, your recordkeeping, and your vendor agreements are all required regardless of how the rules come out. None of that is wasted work. The vendor piece is the one with the longest lead time.

If you are not sure where your business sits, start with the coverage question or work through the SB 26-189 compliance checklist. And if you would rather talk it through than read your way to an answer, I would welcome the conversation.

Common questions

Has the Colorado Attorney General issued rules under SB 26-189?

No. As of August 2026 no rules have been promulgated. The Colorado Department of Law published a pre-rulemaking considerations paper in June 2026 and collected informal public comment through July 13, 2026. Formal notice-and-comment rulemaking under the Colorado Administrative Procedure Act, including draft regulations and at least one public hearing, was expected to begin at the end of summer 2026. The statute requires rules clarifying post-adverse-outcome disclosures on or before January 1, 2027.

What is the Colorado Attorney General asking about in the ADMT rulemaking?

The considerations paper asks how regulations could clarify the definitions of ADMT, materially influence, and consequential decision; how to distinguish ADMTs from technology that simply summarizes, organizes, or presents information; whether the developer and deployer definitions are sufficient and whether other roles should be accounted for; what standards determine whether human review was meaningful; what makes human review commercially reasonable; what sector-specific disclosure guidance would help; and what constitutes clear and conspicuous notice.

Will small businesses get flexibility on human review under Colorado’s AI rules?

It is an open question the Attorney General has explicitly raised. SB 26-189 contains no small business exemption, but the Act conditions meaningful human review on what is commercially reasonable, and the considerations paper asks whether factors including organizational capacity or size, volume of decisions, cost, and technological feasibility should play a role. The Department stated its questions do not indicate any predisposition, so this should not be relied on as expected relief.

What principles will guide the Colorado ADMT rules?

The Department set out five: promote consumer rights, clarify ambiguities, facilitate efficient and expeditious compliance, harmonize with other state, national, and international frameworks, and allow for innovation without unduly burdening entities.

Does the Colorado rulemaking cover the Chatbot Safety Act too?

Yes. The same considerations paper covers House Bill 26-1263, the Chatbot Safety Act, passed May 11, 2026. That Act does not require rulemaking, but the Attorney General intends to issue rules under general discretionary authority to make compliance obligations clear, including the content of the annual report operators must submit.

Regulatory status as of August 1, 2026

This describes what the statute says. The Colorado Attorney General has not yet promulgated rules under SB 26-189, and the rules will matter.

The statute requires the Attorney General to adopt rules clarifying the post-adverse-outcome disclosure requirements on or before January 1, 2027, the same day the law takes effect. The Attorney General’s office published a pre-rulemaking considerations paper in June 2026 and accepted informal public comment through July 13, 2026. That paper asked broader questions than the statutory mandate requires, including whether the regulations should further clarify what counts as automated decision-making technology, what “materially influence” means, and which decisions are “consequential.” Formal notice-and-comment rulemaking, with draft regulations and at least one public hearing, is expected to begin in late summer 2026.

Those are the definitions this page relies on. Expect the rules to add specificity, and in some areas to change how these terms apply in practice. Anything here should be treated as the statutory baseline rather than a final answer, and revisited once the rules issue.
