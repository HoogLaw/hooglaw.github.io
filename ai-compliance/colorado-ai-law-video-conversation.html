Colorado’s AI Law: A Conversation with Michael Devous Jr.

Michael Hoog with Michael Devous Jr., The AI Collective, Colorado Springs  |  11 minutes  |  Recorded November 18, 2026
← Colorado AI Compliance

Recorded November 18, 2026, the day before the Attorney General adopted the final rules. For what changed, see Colorado’s final AI rules.

In short

  • Colorado’s AI law, SB 26-189, takes effect January 1, 2027. It applies when automated decision-making technology materially influences a consequential decision about an identifiable person, in areas such as hiring, housing, lending, insurance, education, and healthcare.
  • There is no small business exemption and no employee or revenue threshold.
  • A business can become a deployer without buying an AI product, for example when a job board’s settings rank or filter applicants and the business works from that list.
  • Start with an inventory of the tools you use, check your job board settings, and plan how people will review decisions.
  • There is no private right of action. Enforcement runs through the Colorado Attorney General.

Michael Devous Jr. is the Founding Organizer of The AI Collective, Colorado Springs chapter. Michael Hoog is a Longmont business attorney who advises Colorado companies on AI governance and compliance.

What changed in the final rules

  • The presumption. Under the final rules, a score, ranking, or recommendation is presumed to have materially influenced a decision when the decision maker reviews it, when it screens what the decision maker sees, or when it decides with no human involved.
  • Rebutting it. A second reviewer helps only if the record shows real independent review: substantial other relevant information, and the authority, subject matter understanding, and time reasonably necessary. A cutoff works if it was documented before the decision and isn’t derived from, calculated by, or dependent on the tool’s output.
  • The covered areas. The statute’s list of consequential decisions also includes insurance and essential government services and public benefits.

What the law covers

Michael Devous Jr.: We’re talking about SB 26-189, Colorado’s Automated Decision-Making Technology Act, which was signed May 14, 2026, and replaced the 2024 Colorado AI Act that never actually took effect. Talk to us a little bit about this. What does the act actually cover, and who will it apply to?

Michael Hoog: The act covers automated decision-making in six areas: education, employment, healthcare, finance, housing, and financial services. One interesting piece, just to put it in context: the Attorney General’s office is promulgating rules around this, and all of that is coming out of its consumer protection division. What the act is really designed to do is protect individuals from decisions made through an automated process, and create some accountability on the employer or organizational side.

There are really three key phrases companies need to understand. It applies where a consequential decision is made that is materially influenced by automated decision-making and affects an identifiable individual.

To break that down, let’s take employment, because that’s one most companies are going to have to deal with. If I apply for a job, I’m an identifiable individual. I, Michael, have applied for that job. It’s not some nebulous person out in the ether. It’s me, Michael, applying for the job. The decision is consequential because it’s a decision about whether I get the job, or the lease on the apartment, or the home loan, or the scholarship, or admission to the private school.

Then “materially influences.” That’s really where the rubber meets the road, and where all the debate is going to be: what constitutes something that materially influences. If you hit all three of those factors, identifiable individual, consequential decision, materially influenced by automated decision-making, it imposes compliance obligations on the organization as a deployer of automated decision-making. That’s really what it comes down to.

“Materially influence” and the presumption

Michael Hoog: I think this one’s worth getting into a little bit, because it really is ground zero for the discussion. The draft rule came out in early October. There was a public hearing on the 26th. There were, I think, 290-something comments from the public. I’ve read close to all of them, probably not all, but close. And materially influence was really at the heart of those discussions. I’m going to go straight from the draft here. The draft rule says an ADMT output is presumed to materially influence a decision if the decision maker saw it or screened what they saw, and the output is consistent with the outcome.

Let’s put that into practical terms. I have a job posting and I get 50 applicants. The screening goes through and gives me the top 10: here are the 10 to interview, ignore these others. I see that output and say, okay, I’m going to interview these top 10 and ignore these. I saw that the automated decision-making told me to ignore them, I do in fact ignore them, and then they get an adverse decision. There is a presumption that the decision was materially influenced by the automated decision-making.

Who counts as a deployer

Michael Devous Jr.: Who counts as a deployer? Does a small business using an off-the-shelf tool, like a plug-and-play productized tool, qualify as a deployer, or is it a little bit more in-depth?

Michael Hoog: Interestingly, you could kind of stumble your way into being a deployer. Number one, there is no floor in terms of number of employees or revenue. There’s no threshold you’ve got to hit.

You and I were talking the other day about things like LinkedIn and Indeed, so I did some digging into their terms of use. Interestingly, Indeed actually references the Colorado law in its terms of use.

Michael Devous Jr.: Wow.

Michael Hoog: So again, the three things are: is it a consequential decision that affects an identifiable individual and is materially influenced by automated decision-making? I don’t have to have ever purchased any type of product. The way I read the law and the rules, and maybe we’ll talk to the AG’s office and they’ll disagree, I could put a posting on LinkedIn or Indeed, and it depends on what settings I have. LinkedIn has some default settings that are different from Indeed’s, and I’m sure every job posting site has its own. But if I’ve got certain settings in place when I post that job and it automatically weeds out certain applicants, or if it gives me a priority ranking and I do a cutoff there myself, then under the plain language of the rules I have used automated decision-making to make a consequential decision, and it’s been materially influenced by an automated decision-making tool, whether I intended to or not. I don’t know if that’s something that was contemplated, but as I read the rule, that is a very fair application of it.

Michael Devous Jr.: So the short answer is no, you don’t have to do anything special. You could accidentally find yourself categorized as a deployer.

Michael Hoog: Yes. Depending on how you’re using certain technologies.

What a small business should do before January

Michael Devous Jr.: What should a one-person shop, a single individual business owner, do before January if they’re trying to make sure they get into compliance, or they’re looking at AI software to use for this?

Michael Hoog: I think the starting point is to take an inventory of whatever tools you’re using. Am I using LinkedIn? Am I using Claude or ChatGPT or Gemini or whatever? And if so, how am I using it? Because that’s the other piece: I could be using ChatGPT and saying, “I’m going to get resumes, but before I ever look at them, I’m going to dump them all into ChatGPT and tell it to rank them,” and spit out the ones I should look at.

Michael Devous Jr.: Yeah.

Michael Hoog: So take an honest look at what you’re using. Check your job board settings. If you’re using LinkedIn or Indeed, check your settings, because I think in most instances you can toggle on and off features that might bring you into this.

The other is to start thinking about what your human protocol is going to be, and what you could do that potentially moves the decision from material influence to a de minimis factor. So yes, I used automated decision-making, but I have a protocol where, despite that, I’ve got two people who look at every resume or every loan, whatever those things are, so it’s a de minimis factor. In the end, it’s going to be up to the AG’s office to decide.

Enforcement: no private lawsuits

Michael Hoog: I think this is important for folks listening to know. This doesn’t give someone who receives an adverse decision a private right of action. So I apply for a job, and an automated decision-making tool bounces me. I don’t have a right to go sue that employer. What I do have a right to is, number one, notice that automated decision-making is going to be used, at the time I apply. Number two, if there’s an adverse decision, within 30 days of that decision I get notice from the would-be employer that says automated decision-making was used, there’s an adverse decision, the decision was made because our automated decision maker scored you below our threshold, and we had two people look at it. And now I’ve met my obligation as an employer.

If I’m not satisfied with that, if Michael’s mad because he didn’t get the job, I can go to the AG’s office and complain. The AG can do an analysis and decide: do I want to enforce? Did the company comply with its obligations? If so, well, I’m sorry you didn’t get the job, but they did everything they were supposed to do. If they didn’t comply, that’s a different story, and that can lead to an enforcement action from the AG’s office. There could be fines.

Why a governance policy helps either way

Michael Hoog: I think companies have to take an honest look at how they’re using AI today and what they anticipate doing with it in the future. I would submit that not only are the regulations going to come more and more quickly, and potentially be more onerous, but companies are going to use AI more and more. If today they’re just using it to screen a resume, tomorrow they’re going to be using it for this, for that, and the other. So start building a compliance program and a policy for how you’re going to use AI: here are the permissible uses, here are the uses that aren’t permitted, and here is how we want to govern AI within our organization. The sooner, the better. Even if you’re not covered by this act right now, having that policy and that governance approach in place is going to better prepare you to adapt when new regulations start coming down the pike that perhaps you are covered by.
