Your Trust Is Useless If You Don't Fund It

My father was a naval aviator and a commercial airline pilot. My brother has been a captain at United Airlines for over thirty years. My youngest son is a first officer at Southwest. Aviation runs in this family the way law apparently runs in families where people make better decisions than I did.

I've spent a lot of time around pilots. Enough to know that the one thing none of them do, not my father, not my brother, not my son, is skip the preflight checklist. Not once. Not because they've only done it a hundred times. Not because they've done it ten thousand times. The checklist is the checklist. You do it every time, on every aircraft, before every flight. The experience doesn't replace it. The experience is why you understand you can't replace it.

I think about that constantly when I'm working on trusts.

The Document Is Not the Plan

A revocable living trust is one of the most useful tools in estate planning. It avoids probate, keeps your affairs private, manages incapacity, and lets you set specific terms for how your assets pass to your family. Done right, it works beautifully.

Done halfway, it's an expensive document that sits in a drawer and does nothing.

The thing most people don't fully absorb when they sign their trust is that signing it is the preflight briefing. Funding it is the actual preflight. And a surprising number of people file the briefing notes and never walk around the plane.

Funding a trust means transferring ownership of your assets into it. Your house needs a new deed conveying title to the trust. Your bank accounts need to be retitled. Your investment accounts need to reflect the trust as owner. Your business interest needs to be assigned. If the asset isn't in the trust, it doesn't pass through the trust. It passes through probate, the exact thing you set up the trust to avoid.

The Plane Is Fueled. The Tanks Are Empty.

My father flew approaches in weather that would ground most pilots. My brother has logged decades of decisions that kept hundreds of people safe. My son is newer to the cockpit but he's been raised to understand that the systems exist for a reason and you don't shortcut them because you're in a hurry or because you did it fine last time.

None of them would file a flight plan, run the briefing, walk out to the aircraft, and then skip the fuel check because the paperwork looked good. The paperwork looking good is not the same thing as being ready to fly. You verify. You confirm. You check the actual state of the actual aircraft against the actual checklist, every time.

A trust that hasn't been funded is a flight plan for an empty plane. Everything is in order on paper. The destination is clear, the route is charted, the weather has been checked. And the aircraft isn't going anywhere because nobody confirmed the tanks.

What Actually Needs to Happen

Funding a trust isn't complicated, but it requires following through on each item. Real property in Colorado transfers into a trust by deed, a new deed conveying the property from you individually to you as trustee of your trust. That deed needs to be recorded with the county clerk and recorder. It doesn't happen automatically when you sign the trust document. Someone has to prepare it, sign it, and record it.

Bank and investment accounts get retitled by contacting the financial institution directly and completing their paperwork. Some institutions are easy about this. Some are not. Either way, it requires an affirmative step on your part or your attorney's part, and it needs to happen for each account.

Business interests, LLC membership interests, shares in a closely held corporation, need to be formally assigned to the trust, and the company's records need to reflect the change. If your operating agreement has restrictions on transfer, those need to be addressed.

Retirement accounts and life insurance generally should not go into the trust directly, those pass by beneficiary designation and have their own tax considerations. But those designations need to be reviewed and kept current, which is its own item on the checklist.

The Checklist Doesn't End at Signing

Pilots don't do one preflight and consider the aircraft cleared for all future flights. Every flight gets its own check. That's not bureaucracy, that's the recognition that things change. Fuel gets used. Weather shifts. Equipment ages.

Your trust needs the same ongoing attention. You buy a new house, it needs to go into the trust. You open a new account, it needs to be titled correctly. You start a new business, the interest needs to be addressed. Life moves, and the trust only covers what's actually in it at the time it matters.

The clients who get the most out of their estate plans are the ones who treat funding as part of the work, not an afterthought to the signing. The ones who end up in probate court despite having a trust are almost always there for the same reason: the document was done and the follow-through wasn't.

My family would call that preflight failure. The outcome is the same either way.
