Estate Planning Is Just Dive Planning With More Paperwork

I'm a divemaster and scuba instructor. I've logged more dives than I can accurately count, in conditions ranging from "lovely" to "what was I thinking." I train other divers, I teach safety, and I work as a safety diver for underwater performances. I spend a lot of time thinking about how not to have things go wrong underwater.

I also spend a lot of time as an attorney helping people put their estate plans together. And at some point I realized these two things are basically the same job with different gear.

Hear me out.

Nobody Gets in Trouble Because of Bad Technique

In my experience, the divers who get into serious trouble aren't the ones with poor form. They're the ones who skipped the planning. Didn't do the pre-dive check. Didn't establish signals with their buddy. Figured everything would probably be fine and didn't talk through what to do if it wasn't.

Estate planning clients who end up in a mess? Same thing. It's almost never complicated circumstances. It's just that nobody got around to it. The will is from 2003 and doesn't reflect two marriages, three kids, and a house. The retirement account still lists an ex-spouse as beneficiary. There's no power of attorney, so when Dad has a stroke, the family has to go to court just to pay his bills.

The water doesn't care that you had good intentions. Neither does probate court.

The Pre-Dive Check

Before any serious dive, you run through the basics. BCD works. Regulator breathes. Air is full. Weights are right. You confirm with your buddy. Takes maybe five minutes. Has saved lives.

The estate planning equivalent is equally basic and almost nobody has done all of it. Do you have a will that reflects your actual life right now? Have you nominated a guardian for your minor children, because without that, a court decides who raises them, without any input from you? Are your beneficiary designations current on your retirement accounts and life insurance?

Under Colorado law, a valid will doesn't require much, a signature and either two witnesses or a notary (C.R.S. § 15-11-502). The barrier isn't legal complexity. It's the same thing that makes people skip the pre-dive check: the comfortable assumption that everything is probably fine.

Usually it is. But "usually" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

Your Dive Buddy

Solo diving exists. It's also widely regarded as a bad idea. Your dive buddy is the person who knows the plan, watches your air, notices when something is off, and handles things if you can't handle them yourself. The whole point is that you've talked through the contingencies before you need them.

In an estate plan, that's your agent under a power of attorney, financial and medical. The person who can step in and manage your affairs if you're incapacitated. Pay your mortgage. Talk to your doctors. Make decisions that can't wait for you to recover.

Colorado's Uniform Power of Attorney Act (C.R.S. § 15-14-701 et seq.) and the medical durable power of attorney statute (C.R.S. § 15-14-506) make these documents straightforward to put in place. Without them, a family member trying to help you during a crisis may have to petition a court for conservatorship or guardianship first. That process takes weeks and costs money, during the exact window when fast and simple would have been nice.

These are not documents for old people. They're documents for anyone who might someday be incapacitated, which is everyone.

The SMB

At the end of a dive, before you ascend, you deploy a surface marker buoy. Bright inflatable tube that goes up on a line ahead of you. Tells the boat crew where you are and where you're coming up. Without it, they're scanning the surface hoping to spot you. With it, they know exactly where to look.

Your estate plan is the SMB. It tells your family where things are, what you wanted, and how to proceed. A will directs your probate estate. A trust passes assets directly to your beneficiaries without court involvement. Beneficiary designations tell financial institutions exactly who gets what and when.

Without any of that, your family is on the surface looking for you. They'll find you eventually, Colorado's intestate succession laws (C.R.S. § 15-11-101 et seq.) provide a legal framework for figuring it out, but it's slower, more expensive, and may not land where you'd have wanted.

Practice the Emergency Before You Need It

Every certified diver learns emergency ascent procedures. Controlled emergency swimming ascent, buoyant emergency ascent, sharing air with a buddy. You practice these in a pool, in calm water, in conditions where nothing is actually wrong, so that if something is actually wrong you're not figuring it out for the first time.

An advance directive is your emergency procedure for medical situations. It tells your doctors and your family what interventions you want and don't want if you can't speak for yourself. Colorado recognizes these under C.R.S. § 15-18-104. Nobody enjoys drafting one. Everyone who has one is glad they did.

The divers who get into the worst trouble are the ones who never practiced the emergencies because they assumed they wouldn't need to. Don't be that diver.
