Do I Need a Will in Colorado?

Most people know they're supposed to have a will and haven't gotten around to it. Whether you need a will in Colorado, and what happens if you don't have one, is a question worth answering clearly. The answer depends on your situation, who you want to receive your property, whether you have minor children, and how your assets are currently titled.

Here's what Colorado law actually does when you die without one, and where a will makes a real difference.

What Happens If You Die Without a Will in Colorado

Dying without a will is called dying intestate. Colorado's intestate succession laws, found in Article 11 of the Colorado Probate Code (C.R.S. § 15-11-101 et seq.), determine who receives your probate estate. These are not default rules you can override later. Once you're gone, they apply automatically.

If you're married with no children, your spouse receives everything from your probate estate. If you're married and all of your children are also your spouse's children, your spouse still receives everything. The situation changes if you have children from a prior relationship. In that case, under C.R.S. § 15-11-102, your spouse receives the first $186,000 (as adjusted for cost of living) plus half of the remaining balance. Your children from the other relationship receive the rest. That division may not be what anyone in your family expected or wanted, and it can create tension that outlasts the grief.

If you're not married, Colorado's intestate succession rules pass your estate to your descendants, then to your parents, then to your siblings and their descendants, and so on through a specific priority order. If you have a long-term partner you're not married to, they receive nothing under intestate succession. Colorado does not recognize common law marriage for purposes of inheritance unless the relationship meets the specific legal requirements for common law marriage, and even then it requires proof.

If you have no surviving relatives who fall within the statutory priority order, your estate escheats to the state of Colorado.

What a Will Actually Controls

A will controls your probate estate, property titled solely in your name with no beneficiary designation. That's an important distinction. A will has no effect on assets that pass by other means: retirement accounts with named beneficiaries, life insurance payable to a named beneficiary, jointly held property with right of survivorship, bank accounts with payable-on-death designations, or assets held in a trust.

For many people whose assets are primarily retirement accounts and a jointly-held home, a will may control relatively little at death. For people who own real property in their name alone, have a sole proprietorship, or have investment accounts without TOD designations, a will matters significantly.

Under Colorado law, a valid will must be signed by the testator and either witnessed by two people who sign in the testator's presence, or notarized (C.R.S. § 15-11-502). Colorado also recognizes holographic wills, handwritten wills signed by the testator, even without witnesses, as long as the material portions are in the testator's handwriting. A handwritten will that meets those requirements is legally valid in Colorado, though it's not advisable for anything complex.

Minor Children

If you have minor children, you need a will. Full stop.

A will is the document through which you nominate a guardian for your minor children if both parents die. Without a nomination, a court decides who raises your children without any direction from you. The court will try to act in the children's best interests, but it's making that determination without knowing your wishes, your family relationships, or your reasons for preferring one person over another.

Under C.R.S. § 15-14-202, a parent can nominate a guardian for a minor child in a will or in a signed written instrument. That nomination doesn't bind the court, but it carries substantial weight. It's also the clearest expression of your intent that exists.

A will also lets you establish a trust for your minor children's inheritance, so that assets don't pass outright to an 18-year-old. Without that planning, a minor who inherits property may require a conservatorship proceeding, a court-supervised process for managing the child's assets until they reach adulthood, which costs money and involves ongoing court oversight.

When Intestate Succession Works Against You

Colorado's intestate succession laws reflect what the legislature decided most people would want. They may not reflect what you want.

Common situations where the default rules cause problems: you're in a second marriage and want your assets to stay with your children from your first marriage; you want to leave something to a sibling, a friend, or a charity; you want to treat your children differently based on their needs or circumstances; you've become estranged from a family member who would inherit under the default rules; or you want to benefit a partner you're not married to.

None of those outcomes happen under intestate succession. A will is the mechanism for expressing them.

What a Will Can't Do

A will cannot override a beneficiary designation. If your life insurance names your ex-spouse and you die with a will leaving everything to your current spouse, the ex-spouse gets the insurance. Colorado's intestate succession laws do have a provision under C.R.S. § 15-11-804 that automatically revokes certain transfers to a former spouse upon divorce, but the interaction between that statute and specific asset types is not always simple. Keeping beneficiary designations current is not optional planning, it's essential.

A will does not avoid probate. If your estate goes through probate, your will is submitted to the court and becomes a public record. Anyone can read it. For people who want privacy or who want to minimize the time and cost of administration, a revocable trust combined with a pour-over will is a more complete solution than a will alone.

So Do You Need One?

If you have minor children: yes, without question. If you have assets titled in your name alone: yes. If you want to direct what happens to your property at death rather than leaving it to the statutory defaults: yes. If your estate is relatively simple, your assets all have beneficiary designations, and your family situation is straightforward: a will may not do much work, but it's still a baseline document that costs little to have and eliminates uncertainty.

The deeper question for most people isn't whether they need a will, it's whether a will alone is enough, or whether a broader plan that includes a trust, powers of attorney, and advance directives would serve them better. Those documents work together, and a will is typically the starting point, not the finish line.
