How to Avoid Probate in Colorado

Probate is the court-supervised process for transferring a deceased person's assets to their heirs. In Colorado, it's governed by the Colorado Probate Code, Title 15 of the Colorado Revised Statutes. The process itself isn't always difficult, but it takes time, it's a matter of public record, and it can be avoided entirely with proper planning. Most people who ask about probate want to know how to avoid probate in Colorado, whether their assets can pass directly to their family without court involvement.

Here's how Colorado law lets you do that.

Revocable Living Trusts

A revocable living trust is the most flexible and comprehensive way to avoid probate in Colorado. You transfer assets into the trust during your lifetime. You remain the trustee and retain full control. When you die, the successor trustee you've named distributes the assets directly to your beneficiaries, with no court filing required.

Under the Colorado Uniform Trust Code (C.R.S. § 15-5-601 et seq.), a revocable trust can be amended or revoked by the settlor at any time during their lifetime. You're not giving anything up. The trust simply changes how ownership is titled so that assets pass outside your estate for probate purposes.

A trust works for nearly every type of asset: real estate, bank accounts, investment accounts, business interests, and personal property. It also lets you set conditions on distributions, provide for a surviving spouse, and plan for incapacity in a way a will cannot. For most families with real property or multiple assets in different categories, a revocable trust is the foundation of a plan that avoids probate entirely.

Beneficiary Deeds

Colorado allows real property to transfer at death through a beneficiary deed, sometimes called a transfer-on-death deed. The statute is found at C.R.S. § 15-15-402. You execute and record the deed during your lifetime, naming who receives the property when you die. The deed has no effect until death, and you can revoke it at any time before then by recording a revocation. The grantee-beneficiary has no rights in the property while you're alive.

On your death, ownership vests directly in the named beneficiary without going through probate (C.R.S. § 15-15-407). The beneficiary doesn't have to do anything other than record an affidavit of survivorship and a certified copy of the death certificate.

A beneficiary deed is a straightforward, low-cost tool for Colorado homeowners who want their residence to pass directly to a child or other beneficiary. It works well as a standalone measure for a single property. For people with more complex situations, multiple properties, a blended family, or concerns about what happens if the named beneficiary dies before you do, a trust typically provides more control and certainty.

Payable-on-Death and Transfer-on-Death Designations

Colorado law (C.R.S. § 15-15-201 et seq.) allows bank accounts and investment accounts to pass directly to named beneficiaries outside of probate through payable-on-death (POD) and transfer-on-death (TOD) designations. You fill out a form with your financial institution, name one or more beneficiaries, and the account transfers automatically at your death. The beneficiary shows the institution a death certificate and receives the funds, no court involved.

The same mechanism applies to brokerage and securities accounts under the Colorado Uniform TOD Security Registration Act (C.R.S. § 15-15-301 et seq.). You can register securities in beneficiary form, and ownership transfers on death to the named beneficiary.

Retirement accounts and life insurance policies work the same way through beneficiary designations maintained with the plan administrator or insurer, not through your will or a court process.

Keeping these designations current matters. An outdated designation naming a former spouse or a deceased person can create exactly the kind of complication that sends an estate into probate anyway.

Joint Tenancy

Holding property in joint tenancy with right of survivorship means that when one owner dies, the surviving joint tenant automatically owns the entire property. It passes by operation of law, not through probate. Colorado recognizes this for both real property and personal property.

Joint tenancy is simple and effective in the right situation, typically a married couple with straightforward intentions. It becomes problematic when the joint tenant predeceases you, when there are multiple children involved, or when you want different assets to go to different people. It also carries gift tax implications if you add someone other than a spouse to title. It's worth understanding what you're creating before assuming joint tenancy is the right solution.

Small Estates

Colorado has a simplified process for small estates that don't require full probate. Under C.R.S. § 15-12-1201, if the total value of personal property subject to probate doesn't exceed $74,000 (as adjusted for cost of living, this figure is updated periodically), an heir can collect that property by presenting an affidavit to the holder. No court proceeding is required.

For small estates with only personal property and no real estate, this can make a formal probate proceeding unnecessary. But it doesn't work for real property, and it only applies if the estate genuinely falls under the threshold. It's not a planning tool so much as a relief valve for modest estates.

What Doesn't Avoid Probate

A will does not avoid probate. A will is a set of instructions to the probate court. It tells the court who gets what, but it doesn't bypass the process. If your estate consists of assets titled solely in your name with no beneficiary designations, those assets go through probate regardless of whether you have a will.

The goal of probate avoidance planning is to make sure assets either have named beneficiaries or are held in a trust, so that nothing is left in your name alone at death with nowhere to go but court.

Getting the Planning Right

Each of these tools works differently, and they work best in combination. A revocable trust can hold real estate and investment accounts. A beneficiary deed can handle a cabin or a rental property. POD designations cover bank accounts. A pour-over will picks up anything that didn't make it into the trust. The result is an estate that passes quickly, privately, and without court supervision.
