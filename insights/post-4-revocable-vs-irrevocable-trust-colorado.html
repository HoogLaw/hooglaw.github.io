Revocable vs. Irrevocable Trust in Colorado

The difference between a revocable and irrevocable trust comes down to one thing: whether you can take it back. That single distinction drives almost everything else, how the trust is taxed, whether it protects assets from creditors, and what it can and can't accomplish for your estate plan.

Most people asking this question are trying to figure out which one they need. The answer for the majority of Colorado families is a revocable trust. But understanding why requires knowing what each one actually does.

Revocable Trusts

A revocable trust, often called a revocable living trust or an inter vivos trust, is one you create and fund during your lifetime, with the right to amend or revoke it at any time. Under the Colorado Uniform Trust Code, C.R.S. § 15-5-602, the settlor of a revocable trust retains the power to revoke or amend the trust throughout their lifetime. That power ends at death.

Because you retain control, the trust is essentially transparent for tax purposes while you're alive. The IRS treats the assets as still belonging to you. You report trust income on your personal return. There's no separate trust tax return during the settlor's lifetime for a standard revocable trust.

What a revocable trust accomplishes: it changes how assets are titled so they pass outside of probate at your death, transfers to your successor trustee without court involvement if you become incapacitated, and lets you set specific instructions for how assets are managed and distributed. It also keeps your affairs private, unlike a will, a trust isn't filed with the court and doesn't become a public record.

What a revocable trust does not accomplish: it provides no asset protection. Because you can revoke it and take everything back, your creditors can reach the assets too. The trust offers no Medicaid planning benefit for the same reason. If asset protection is the goal, a revocable trust is the wrong tool.

Irrevocable Trusts

An irrevocable trust is one you generally cannot change or undo after you create it. You transfer assets into it, and you give up ownership and control. Under C.R.S. § 15-5-411, an irrevocable trust can be modified or terminated only under specific circumstances, with the consent of the settlor and all beneficiaries, or by court order for reasons like unanticipated circumstances or changed conditions that make the trust's purpose impractical.

Colorado also adopted the Uniform Trust Decanting Act (C.R.S. § 15-16-901 et seq.), which gives trustees certain powers to modify an irrevocable trust by distributing assets into a new trust with different terms, within limits. Decanting has made irrevocable trusts more flexible than they used to be, but the basic principle still holds: you're giving up control in exchange for benefits you can't get with a revocable trust.

Those benefits typically include one or more of the following.

Asset protection. Because you no longer own the assets, a properly structured irrevocable trust can shield them from your future creditors, including, in some cases, Medicaid spend-down requirements. Colorado's spendthrift trust provisions (C.R.S. § 15-5-502) allow a trust to restrict a beneficiary's ability to transfer their interest and limit creditors' access to trust assets, subject to certain exceptions.

Estate tax planning. Assets transferred to an irrevocable trust are generally removed from your taxable estate. For people with estates large enough to face federal estate tax exposure, irrevocable trusts, including irrevocable life insurance trusts, grantor retained annuity trusts, and charitable trusts, are core planning tools. For most Colorado families, the federal estate tax threshold is high enough that this isn't the primary driver.

Specific purpose trusts. Some irrevocable trusts exist for a defined purpose rather than general estate planning. A special needs trust (sometimes structured as irrevocable) holds assets for a beneficiary with a disability without disqualifying them from means-tested government benefits. A charitable remainder trust provides income to the settlor or other beneficiaries with the remainder passing to charity. These are purpose-built tools for specific situations.

The Practical Difference for Most Families

For a Colorado family with a home, retirement accounts, savings, and a straightforward intention to pass assets to a spouse and then children, a revocable living trust handles the job. It avoids probate, manages incapacity, keeps things private, and gives you control throughout your lifetime. It's the right starting point for most estate plans.

An irrevocable trust enters the picture when there's a specific problem a revocable trust can't solve: protecting assets from creditors, planning for a beneficiary with special needs, reducing a taxable estate, or providing for a blended family in a way that locks in certain outcomes. These are situations where giving up control is worth it because of what you get in return.

The two aren't mutually exclusive. A complete estate plan sometimes includes both, a revocable trust as the primary vehicle, and one or more irrevocable trusts addressing specific issues alongside it.

A Note on Modification

The line between revocable and irrevocable has become somewhat less rigid under modern Colorado trust law. Court modification, decanting, and non-judicial settlement agreements (C.R.S. § 15-5-111) all provide pathways to change an irrevocable trust in the right circumstances. But those are exception mechanisms, not the rule. You shouldn't go into an irrevocable trust assuming it can be easily changed later.

Which One Do You Need?

That depends on what you're trying to accomplish. If the goal is probate avoidance, privacy, and a clean transition of assets at death, a revocable trust is the answer. If there's a creditor concern, a taxable estate, or a beneficiary with special circumstances, the conversation becomes more specific.
