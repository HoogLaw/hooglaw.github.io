Colorado Probate Process Timeline

When someone dies owning assets in their name alone, those assets typically have to go through probate before they can be transferred to heirs. People going through this for the first time usually want to know two things: how long does probate take in Colorado, and what actually happens during that time.

The honest answer to the first question is that most Colorado probates close within six to twelve months. Some close faster. Some take longer, particularly if there are creditor issues, disputes among heirs, or real property that needs to be sold. Here's what drives the timeline.

Opening the Estate

Probate begins when someone petitions the court to open the estate and appoint a personal representative, the person who will administer it. In Colorado, this can be done informally or formally.

Informal probate (C.R.S. § 15-12-301 et seq.) is handled by the court registrar rather than a judge. It's faster and less expensive, and it's appropriate for most straightforward estates. The registrar reviews the application and, if the requirements are met, issues Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration. Those letters give the personal representative legal authority to act on behalf of the estate.

Formal probate (C.R.S. § 15-12-401 et seq.) involves a hearing before a judge. It's used when there's a dispute about the will, a question about who should serve as personal representative, or other circumstances that require court involvement. Formal proceedings take longer and cost more.

Opening the estate typically takes a few weeks from the date of filing if it's uncontested and the paperwork is in order.

Notice to Creditors

After the estate is opened, the personal representative must publish a notice to creditors in a newspaper of general circulation in the county where the estate is pending (C.R.S. § 15-12-801). Creditors then have four months from the date of first publication to present their claims against the estate.

This four-month creditor claim period is the primary reason most Colorado probates can't close in less than five or six months. Even if everything else is done, the estate has to wait out that window before it's safe to distribute assets to heirs.

Known creditors, people or entities the personal representative is aware of, must also receive direct notice. The personal representative has a duty to identify and notify them, not just rely on publication.

Inventory and Appraisal

Within three months of appointment, the personal representative must prepare an inventory of the estate's assets and their fair market values (C.R.S. § 15-12-706). For most estates, this means gathering account statements, getting real property appraised if necessary, and documenting what exists. The inventory isn't filed with the court in informal proceedings, but it has to be prepared and provided to interested persons upon request.

If the estate includes a business interest, real property, or assets that are hard to value, this step can take longer and may require hiring an appraiser.

Paying Debts and Taxes

Before distributing anything to heirs, the personal representative pays the estate's valid debts and expenses in the priority order set out in C.R.S. § 15-12-805. That order puts costs of administration first, then funeral expenses, then certain family allowances, then debts and taxes.

Colorado does not have a state estate tax. The federal estate tax applies to estates above the federal exemption amount, which is well above the threshold for most Colorado families. For estates that do have federal tax obligations, the return is due nine months from the date of death, with a possible six-month extension. Tax issues can extend the timeline significantly.

Distribution and Closing

Once the creditor claim period has passed, debts are paid, and any tax matters are resolved, the personal representative distributes the remaining assets to the heirs or beneficiaries as directed by the will, or by the intestate succession statutes if there's no will.

The estate can be closed informally by the personal representative filing a sworn statement (C.R.S. § 15-12-1003) confirming that administration is complete. There's no court hearing required in most informal proceedings. The personal representative's authority ends, and the estate is done.

For formal proceedings, closing requires a court order.

What Can Extend the Timeline

Several things push probate past the typical six-to-twelve-month window. Real property that needs to be sold adds time, particularly in a slow market. Disputes among heirs or challenges to the will require formal proceedings and often litigation. Creditor claims that need to be evaluated or contested take additional time. Estates with income tax issues for the decedent's final return, or with complex assets like business interests, require more administration before closing.

Colorado also has a three-year statute of limitations on reopening an estate after informal closing (C.R.S. § 15-12-1008), which occasionally becomes relevant when later-discovered assets come to light.

Supervised vs. Unsupervised Administration

Most Colorado probates proceed without ongoing court supervision. The personal representative administers the estate, makes decisions, and closes it without going back to the court at each step. Supervised administration (C.R.S. § 15-12-501 et seq.) is available but rarely used, it's typically reserved for situations where there's reason to believe the personal representative needs oversight, or where an interested party requests it.

Unsupervised administration gives the personal representative more flexibility and keeps costs down. It also puts more responsibility on the personal representative to get things right.

If You're Serving as Personal Representative

Being named in a will as personal representative doesn't mean you have to navigate this alone. The role carries legal duties and potential personal liability if those duties aren't met. Most personal representatives work with an attorney, at least for the opening and closing of the estate and for guidance on creditor claims and distributions.
